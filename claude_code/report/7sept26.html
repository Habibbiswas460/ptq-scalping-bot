What the day was supposed to be

This was booked as the out-of-sample test of the project's strongest result: that the 09:15–09:45 window, which the time filter had always blocked, returned +₹114.06/trade against −₹34.41 for the traded day. The time gate was opened for this session specifically so that window could finally be traded.

It was never traded. The bot placed its first order at 10:16:01, thirty-one minutes after the window closed, and the reasons it could not are the subject of the next section. The scheduled experiment did not happen; a different one did.

Chronology

Six blockers, each hidden by the one in front

None of these are individually unreasonable. Every one of them is a mechanism someone added deliberately. What no one chose is their conjunction — and because each returned silently, only the frontmost was ever visible.

  1. The PE trend-exhaustion filter

    986 of 986 evaluations rejected before scoring · 0 scored signals in 30 minutes

    A hardcoded rule: rsi < 30 and macd_hist rising = "bounce coming, do not buy PE". In a downtrend at RSI 17.7 it fired on every evaluation. The session produced not one scored signal. EXP-11 could show neither that this gate earns its rejections nor that it costs anything, so the standing decision had been "no action" — which is why it had never been tested. Now settable; the PE half was disabled at 09:28:35.

  2. Indicators rebuilt from nothing — self-inflicted

    EMA9/EMA21 separated by 0.1–0.7 pts against 32 pts · 17 minutes lost

    My own restart hit the broker's historical-candle rate limit, so warm-up logged "indicators will start from zero" and the EMAs were rebuilt from two minutes of ticks instead of 5-minute candles. The chop filter then rejected everything. The existing fallback cache was in-memory, so it was empty in the only case that matters — process startup. It is now written to disk, and it caught the same failure twice more the same afternoon.

  3. The opposite direction was always the wrong moneyness

    988 × "Premium too low ₹32 < ₹70" against 512 confidence rejections

    The subscribed strike is chosen to put the subscribed option type inside the ₹70–350 premium band. Both the validation path and the order path reused that same strike for the opposite direction — and a strike that makes a CE in-the-money makes the PE out-of-the-money by the same distance. Holding an ITM 23800 CE in a falling market, every PE the strategy asked for priced at ₹31–32. This is the most plausible reason the entire tick record holds 297 PE rows against ~73,000 CE.

  4. Open interest could never have populated

    154 tick rows at oi = 0; 73,171 NULLs historically; never a positive value

    Option tokens are subscribed with a hardcoded literal 2 in eight places — WS_MODE_QUOTE. Open interest lives at bytes 131:139 of a mode-3 SnapQuote packet and is simply absent from a Quote packet, so the flag enabled two days earlier had been reading a field the subscription never asked for. The same gap explains the fabricated spread: bytes 147:347 are the best-5 book, and the parser jumped from 139 straight to 347.

  5. Two losses paused the session for thirty minutes, not fifteen

    Streak at 10:19:44 → first evaluation 10:49:47. After the fix: 11:32:30 → 11:47:32

    The risk manager armed its pause the first time it observed a streak, not when the streak happened — and while the state machine sat in its own 900-second cooldown it never reached the check at all. The two clocks ran back to back instead of together. This had been fixed once before, by deleting a duplicate pause; the duplication survived through the cooldown duration instead.

  6. Recovery mode was a permanent trading halt

    87 consecutive accepted signals placed no order · 12:02 → 13:53

    NIFTY's minimum position is one 65-lot, which at an 8-point stop risks ₹520 against a ₹900 per-trade budget — so any size multiplier below 0.578 computes a position that cannot be executed. Recovery mode's configured "size 50%" lands at 0.4936. It does not halve the trade; there is no half lot. Worse, recovery_mode was persisted and recovery_start_date was not, and the exit condition is guarded on that date — so after any restart the mode could be entered and never left. Trading is the only way out of recovery, and recovery blocked trading.

The pattern, not the list

The failure mode here is not wrong logic. Each mechanism is defensible on its own. It is that they compose multiplicatively and return silently — return "IDLE" with nothing logged looks exactly like a market with no signals. The most useful changes made today were not logic but logging: the entry-hold reason, and the sizing arithmetic. Without those two lines, blockers 5 and 6 would still be invisible.

The seventh thing

Why three months produced nothing

Every P&L figure this project has ever produced is gross. There is no brokerage, STT, exchange transaction charge, GST or stamp duty anywhere in the codebase — the bot computes (exit − entry) × qty and stops. There is also no backtest artifact behind the strategy docstring's claim of "+42.2% monthly".

Out-of-sample

Both of the morning's leads failed by evening

Before the market opened I re-derived the project's flagship result on the audited layer and it reproduced: the opening window at +₹151.26 against −₹30.42, p=0.0165. I then found a mechanism for it — realised range, not the clock — where a 30-point gate moved expectancy from −₹32.26 to +₹72.02 inside the traded day, p=0.042. I flagged at the time that the threshold was chosen on the same two sessions that scored it, across roughly 21 threshold/lookback combinations, and that the p-value was not corrected for that.

So the fabrication sat at the top of the real range — a reasonable guess, slightly pessimistic, worth about ₹8 per round trip. That refines the synthetic-bid/ask correction rather than overturning it: the spread cost was invented, but it was not badly invented, and it is not where the expectancy problem lives. One contract, two minutes, one time of day — opening spreads remain uncharacterised.

ticks.oi carried real values for the first time (1,646,840, moving) against 73,171 historical NULLs. The scorer still could not use them: update_oi_data() classifies from the change between two consecutive ticks against a ±1% threshold, and the broker republishes OI in steps — 243 of 246 comparisons were exactly zero, one crossed the threshold. Over the same window OI drifted −1.29%, which is the signal the rule was written to read. A time-baseline option is written and defaulted off.

The bot's loss is three times more friction-dominated than the average losing retail trader's, because it trades one lot at a flat ₹40 per round trip, very often, on a ₹30,000 account. It also sits squarely in the cohort SEBI identifies as worst-performing: option buying (97% of individual traders; 87.7% of them lost money in FY26), small capital, high turnover.

Gross P&L across 144 trades is −₹12.28 per trade — statistically indistinguishable from zero. The strategy has no measured edge, and it pays ₹63.80 a trade for the privilege of not having one. That is the whole result: not a tuning problem, a frequency problem.

What this does not establish

  • Today is not a controlled experiment. Five settings changed inside the session and the bot was restarted six times. Switch times are recorded in logs/2026-09-07/SWITCH_pe_exhaustion_off.txt; nothing here should be attributed to any single one of them.
  • The excursion figures are n=37 across three sessions, and they are the bot's own entries rather than a head-to-head against arbitrary timing.
  • The real-spread measurement is one contract over two minutes. It does not characterise the open, other strikes, or other days.
  • Costs are Zerodha's published rates and reflect the Budget 2026 STT rise to 0.15%. A different broker changes the arithmetic; the model takes them as constructor arguments for that reason.
  • Four restarts reset the daily loss counter before that was fixed, so the bot's own Daily: figure for today is wrong. Every number in this report is computed from the trades table, not from that counter.

What follows from it

  1. Re-read every prior experiment as gross. EXP-01 through EXP-11 are all stated before costs and are therefore about ₹64/trade too optimistic, in the same direction. "X3 at −₹24.86 beats control at −₹40.05" compares two numbers both far under water once the trade is paid for.
  2. Stop tuning the exit ladder. Four experiments have now swept both sides of it. Against a signal whose forward distribution is adverse-skewed, no exit policy closes the gap, and the fast loss cut it already uses is the correct response.
  3. The next question is the entry, and it has to clear a bar that is now known: an average win above 3.94 points even at eight lots, 5.30 at one. Nothing measured so far comes close to 2.45.
  4. Trade far less often, or not at this size. With a near-zero gross edge and ₹63.80 of friction, trade count is the dominant term in the P&L and it currently runs the wrong way.
  5. Deferred deliberately: the recovery-mode trigger reads all-time total_pnl rather than drawdown from peak, so accumulated lifetime losses will cross 8% once and latch. It needs its own change and its own measurement.
